# Scenery as Weapon

*Land, absence, and the work of proving presence*

Where a name should be, there is a square.

In the West Bengal electoral documents I have been working through, some name fields contain only repeated marks for an undefined character. The square occupies the place of a letter without preserving it. Looking harder will not bring the letter back. Nor will a better machine learn to read what the document no longer contains. The electoral identifier survives. The name has become unreadable.[^names]

There are 6,924 such damaged cells across electors' and relatives' names; another 55 are empty. A cell is a space allotted to a name, sometimes to a relationship. Its damage leaves the surrounding record intact. The columns still line up. The page retains the appearance of order.

That appearance is where I want to begin. A document can look complete while something necessary to a life has fallen out of it. So can a landscape. The field remains in the photograph after the labourer has left the frame; a settlement becomes an obstruction in a plan that has no place for its history. Scenery acquires authority through what it allows us to stop asking about.

This book follows the separation of land from the relations that sustain it, and of language from the lives in which it grows. The damaged name gives that separation a material form. It also gives us a difficult task: to attend to an absence without making the person behind it disappear into our metaphor.

## The failed encounter

The documents belong to the 2025 enumeration for West Bengal's Special Intensive Revision, or SIR, of the electoral roll for 2026. They are grouped under ASD—absent, shifted, or dead—with an additional category for those already enrolled elsewhere. They record forms described as “uncollectable”; the official portal identifies the associated lists as electors present in the 2025 rolls but omitted from the SIR 2026 draft.[^stage]

*Uncollectable* describes the outcome of an attempt to obtain a form. Read too quickly, it becomes a description of the person from whom the form was sought. A failed encounter becomes a stable attribute. The word leaves us with the result, detached from the circumstances of the encounter: who looked, where, when, and what would have enabled the form to return.

My extraction contains 5,819,543 records. Their recorded reasons break down as follows: death, 2,416,131; permanently shifted, 1,987,636; untraceable or absent, 1,277,521; already enrolled, 138,255.[^data] Before proceeding, the boundary of this evidence should be clear. These are records of administrative classifications and draft omissions, not 5.8 million established cases of final disenfranchisement. They do not independently verify the reasons, establish deliberate targeting, or tell us what happened after a claim. Damage to a name does not establish an intention to erase it. The argument concerns what this process asks of people and what its categories leave out.

Death is the largest category: 41.52 percent. Any serious criticism has to face that fact. People die, move, and acquire duplicate entries; a roll that could never be corrected would fail the living electorate too. The strongest defence of revision is therefore substantial: an accurate roll requires maintenance, and removing an obsolete entry can be a legitimate part of it. The Election Commission describes the exercise in terms of including eligible electors and excluding ineligible entries.[^procedure]

The dispute begins where that purpose is treated as sufficient proof of the process's fairness. Who has to do the work when an eligible person's entry is missing? What must they produce, and how is an official classification made answerable to their account? In its December 2025 instructions, the Chief Electoral Officer directed anyone missing from the draft to submit Form 6 with a declaration and other documents. The same note described official assistance, camps, and avenues for submitting applications.[^procedure] Assistance matters. So does the demand that remains: the omitted person has to undertake a claim to inclusion.

Here, “recognition” has a precise meaning: being accepted by the electoral administration as an elector attached to a place. SIR makes an existing entry subject to renewed enumeration. When that process leaves someone out, restoring the entry requires further work. The burden of making presence administratively legible reaches back into the person's life. Whether that work is easy, onerous, or impossible cannot be settled by the neatness of the resulting list.

## The address and the ground

Nearly two million records are classified as *স্থায়ীভাবে স্থানান্তরিত*: permanently shifted. The phrase brings land back into the roll. It concerns a person's relationship to a place, rendered as a completed movement away from it.

The record gives the movement no history. It does not say whether a person moved for work, followed family, lost a dwelling, or chose another home. Those possibilities require different accounts, but they arrive in the same column. The word *permanently* closes a question that the record gives the reader little means to reopen.

An address condenses a way of inhabiting land into a location at which a person can be found. To live somewhere is also to use paths, depend on water, share work, care for others, and carry names for places that may never appear in an official field. These relations can extend across more than one address. A form needs a determinate entry. The political question is what happens when the entry is allowed to stand for the whole relation.

This book places colonial property regimes, caste power, enclosure, and the destruction of commons within the history of that reduction. Its phrase “legality becomes a weapon” names a danger within the authority to decide which relations count.[^book] Electoral administration meets this terrain through residence. The terms on which a place can be documented matter most sharply where a life cannot easily supply the stability the document expects. That is the connection between the roll and land: political participation is administered through a location, while the conditions of remaining locatable are lived on the ground.

*খুঁজে পাওয়া যায়নি / অনুপস্থিত*: not found / absent. The slash is small enough to overlook. Yet it joins two different statements. “Not found” reports the outcome of a search. *অনুপস্থিত*, absent, names a person's relation to a particular here and now. Absent from where? At what time? For how long? The ordinary word invites these questions. The combined administrative category holds the two conditions together without answering them.

I hear that gap in the Bengali. A person can be absent from a room and still belong to the household. An absence can be temporary even when the person searching cannot explain it. This is a reading of the word, not testimony about any one elector. It matters because the category's compression allows a statement about an encounter to travel as a statement about a life.

For more than a million records, that compression is the reason available to the reader. The document does not follow the person beyond it. The work of finding out what happened afterwards has to begin elsewhere—with a later roll, a decision, a conversation. Administrative knowledge ends much sooner than its confident arrangement on the page suggests.

## Revising

The “-ing” of this book belongs here too: enumerating, finding, classifying, revising. *Special Intensive Revision* names an operation. Its published roll gives that operation the appearance of a finished object. Behind it remain visits and missed encounters, submitted forms and demands for further evidence, classifications and attempts to contest them.

This is where I locate the force of SIR: it reopens the administrative question of who belongs on the roll and requires lives already entered there to become legible again. Correction and the possibility of exclusion occur within the same operation. A defence of accurate records has to account for both, including the work required of an eligible person to undo an omission. Counting successful entries alone cannot answer that question.

A certified film offers a brief parallel. When a scene has been removed, a viewer may encounter a smooth sequence with no sign of the instruction that altered it. The public object conceals part of its making. With the roll, too, the completed surface can draw attention away from the decisions behind it. The consequences differ; the problem of the frame remains. To look only at what has been authorised is to let the authorising process disappear.

Scenery becomes a weapon when this disappearance helps to close an argument. A list's tidiness comes to stand for the justice of the revision. A boundary comes to stand for the legitimacy of possession. People who contest the result then appear as disturbances to an order already achieved. The violence of the frame lies in its capacity to make the conditions of its own production seem irrelevant.

That claim does not require every entry to be wrong. It requires us to resist making a correctable administrative record the final account of the people it describes. A roll can need revision, and the conduct of that revision can remain a matter of political struggle.

## Keeping the place open

To work with these records is to acquire another power over their contents. I can make a table cleaner by dropping a damaged row. I can translate a label until it sounds more definite than the source. I can draw a map whose continuous colour conceals holes in the material from which it was made. Each choice would offer the reader a more settled view.

I want the damaged cell to interrupt that settlement. Keeping it in the record preserves both the entry and the fact that something has been lost. Keeping the Bengali beside the translation leaves the wording available for disagreement. Keeping a route back to the page allows someone else to question what I have counted. These practices give the work a use beyond the authority of its author.

They also place a limit on exposure. The reader does not need a stranger's electoral identifier or a relative's name to understand the aggregate. A method can be open to scrutiny while the people within it retain space outside the public display. The ambition is to make the administration answerable without turning its subjects into another spectacle.

This book's account of sovereignty begins in shared care and continuity: the work through which people sustain a place and one another. The square cannot hold those relations. Neither could the name, had every letter survived. But a name can be spoken, answered, corrected; it can connect a line in a document to a person able to contest what has been written about them. The damaged cell marks a break in that connection.

I return to the page. The columns are straight. The identifier is readable. Where the name should be, the square remains. I leave it visible, with the route back to its source, as an unfinished demand upon the record. Beyond its border is someone whom this page cannot finish describing.

The task is not to recover a pure view. It is to break the view's authority.
